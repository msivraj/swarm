01

The requirement that forces the whole design

The brief asks for three things at once: machines that are tightly coupled, a fleet that scales toward one million, and dispatch that stays low latency. Taken literally and flat, that trio is not buildable — each pair fights the third.

Constraint A

Tight coupling

Machines share state, barriers, or a leader. Coordination has a real per-link cost.

Constraint B

Scale to 1M

All-to-all is O(N²) — a million nodes is 10¹² links. Global consensus caps at ~5–7 voters.

Constraint C

Low latency

Coordinated global decisions can't also be fast. The speed of light is the ceiling.

A + B + C, applied to one flat topology → impossible

The escape hatch every large tightly-coupled system reaches for is the same: confine the tight coupling to bounded groups, and make the groups loosely coupled to each other. Those groups are cells. Tight coupling lives inside a cell; cells barely talk. Growing from ten thousand machines to a million just means more cells — coordination cost per node stays flat. Everything below follows from that one move.

02

Topology

Four parts, and only four: a control plane that plans work, cells that run it, gossip for membership and health inside each cell, and async messages between cells. That is the irreducible core — nothing else is required to hit the requirements.

Why gossip inside, a planner on top

Gossip — specifically SWIM, the protocol behind Serf, Consul, and Cassandra — is genuinely fast and carries constant per-node overhead, but only for the job it's good at: membership, failure detection, and spreading capacity metadata. A new machine joins the swarm by gossiping itself in; no central registration bottleneck. That is your dynamic scaling at the membership layer.

What gossip must not own is authoritative task assignment. Tight coordination needs someone to decide — a barrier to release, a leader to elect — so the queue is fed by a planner: submitters hand in high-level jobs, the planner decomposes them into tasks and routes them to the right cell, and workers pull. Centralized planning, decentralized dispatch — the hot path has no central bottleneck.

03

Mitosis — how cells scale

Cells do not continuously resize to chase an optimum. Rebalancing a tightly-coupled cell means tearing down and rebuilding its coordination state — the single most expensive operation in the system — so you do it rarely, and only at thresholds. A cell has a target size T; it splits when it grows past 2T and two neighbors merge when together they fall below T. The gap between those two triggers is a 2× hysteresis band, wide enough that a freshly-split cell can never immediately re-merge.

Starting thresholds

Size T off the coupling tightness — the tighter the coordination, the smaller the cell, because barrier latency grows with N and the slowest member gates everyone. Begin with counts; once running, graduate the trigger from a count to a measured signal (e.g. split when the cell's barrier-completion p99 crosses your latency SLO).

  • R2New joins fill, then divide. Machines gossip in and land in cells until one crosses 2T and splits. That is the dynamic scaling — no global optimizer chasing "optimum."

05

Substrate stays fixed; the driver plugs in per job

The flavor of coordination belongs to the job, not the platform — a Monte Carlo run shares nothing, training needs a barrier, a graph job wants supersteps. So a cell isn't born with a personality. Every cell runs the same permanent substrate; what the leader actually executes is a coordination driver loaded from the job's coupling field for that job's lifetime. The cell is the room; the job brings its own coordination style and packs it up when it leaves.

The four drivers differ most in the two things that actually constrain a design: what a failure costs and how big the cell can get. That pairing is really what a job is choosing between.

07

Cell internals: the barrier driver

Barrier is the hardest driver and the one the flagship workloads (dist-training, sci-sim) depend on, so it's the reference protocol. A barrier cell runs the same substrate as any other — gossip, a Raft leader, a worker pool — but its leader loads a barrier coordinator that marches every worker through one strict lockstep loop and owns the checkpoint that makes failure survivable.

The step loop

The coordinator drives one iteration at a time. Every worker computes step N on its own data shard, then reports done plus its partial result. The coordinator holds the release until the last worker checks in, all-reduces the partials into the shared data (averaged gradients, exchanged boundaries), and only then releases everyone into step N+1. Nothing races ahead; the whole cell is always on the same step number. Every K steps it pauses to persist shard state to durable storage.

The straggler is the tax you always pay

Because the barrier waits for the slowest, one slow machine slows the entire cell every step — this is the reason barrier cells stay small (the more members, the higher the odds one is slow, and the longer the tail). The coordinator sets a per-step deadline: a worker that misses it isn't waited on indefinitely, it's declared dead by gossip and handled as a failure. That converts an unbounded hang into a bounded, recoverable event.

Failure and recovery

A worker lost mid-step makes that step's partial results incomplete, so the step is invalid — there is no partial-credit in lockstep. Recovery is always the same: restore the last checkpoint, re-partition the shard across the surviving (or replacement) members, and replay forward. If the leader is the one lost, Raft elects a new leader from within the cell and it resumes from the replicated checkpoint metadata. The checkpoint cadence K is the one real tuning knob: smaller K means less recomputation after a failure but more overhead in the steady state.

Where mitosis meets lockstep

This is the collision flagged back in section 03 (rule R1), now made concrete. A member can't join or leave mid-step without corrupting the barrier group, so split and merge happen only at a checkpoint boundary: the coordinator quiesces at the next checkpoint, persists state, the cell divides, each half reloads its slice of the shard, and both resume from that checkpoint. Growing a running job's membership reshapes the data partition the same way — also a checkpoint-boundary operation, never a live one.

  • B1Bound the barrier with a per-step deadline. A missed deadline is a failure to recover from, not a wait to endure — otherwise one hung worker stalls the cell forever.
  • B2Checkpoint every K steps. It's the only resume point a lockstep job has; K trades steady-state overhead against recompute-on-failure.
  • B3Resize only at a checkpoint boundary. Quiesce, snapshot, divide or grow, reload shards, resume — never swap a member mid-step.
  • B4All members present before step 0. Gang admission is mandatory here — start with a partial group and it deadlocks at the very first barrier, waiting on peers that were never scheduled.
08

Cell internals: the leader driver

The leader driver runs coordinated rounds (BSP supersteps) with one authority holding the global state. It powers graph-compute. Unlike barrier, the workers aren't symmetric peers — the leader hands out work and folds results, so its failure story is asymmetric.

  • L1Only the leader's state is precious. Replicate and checkpoint it; followers hold nothing that can't be recomputed from a reassignment.
  • L2Membership changes between rounds. The leader rebalances assignments at a round boundary — cheaper than barrier because the leader mediates the reshape.
09

Cell internals: the message-passing driver

The message-passing driver is the actor model: each worker has an address and a mailbox, sends messages to specific peers, and reacts to what arrives. There is no global barrier, no central authority, and no shared memory — progress is emergent. It powers agent-sim.

  • M1Design messages idempotent and order-tolerant. There is no global ordering; a redelivered or out-of-order message must not corrupt state.
  • M2Failure is local. A supervisor restarts one actor from its snapshot — no global rollback, so cells can stay large.
10

Cell internals: the independent driver

The default, and the simplest: no driver at all. The cell is a pull queue, workers grab tasks that reference nothing but their own input, and results are collected at the end. It powers five of the nine library templates — the bread-and-butter of large-scale compute.

  • I1Make tasks idempotent and re-runnable. Recovery is re-queue-and-retry, so a task that runs twice must produce one correct result.
  • I2The only shared step is the final merge. If work needs to coordinate before the end, it isn't independent — it belongs on a heavier driver.

Tech stack

The stance is reuse proven libraries for the hard distributed primitives, build only what's unique to Swarm. Go for the control plane, agent, and CLI — it's I/O-bound orchestration and the whole primitive ecosystem is already Go-native. Rust on the hot path: the WASM host and the worker data path, where throughput and latency matter. Both cross-compile to every OS and architecture, so the agent ships as one native binary per platform.

13

The control plane is regional

A single control plane cannot hold a million machines — its state store and scheduler would become the exact bottleneck the cell design exists to avoid. So the control plane shards by region, mirroring the fleet: each region owns its own planner, rendezvous, and cell registry, and regions coordinate only loosely and asynchronously. A thin global layer handles cross-region routing and an aggregate, eventually-consistent view — never strongly-consistent state on the hot path.

  • A1No global strongly-consistent state on the hot path. A region schedules from its own store; the global layer is eventually consistent and off the critical path.
  • A2A region is a blast-radius boundary. A region failing takes its own cells, not the fleet — and its agents re-home to a healthy region.
14

The cross-OS agent and NAT-friendly networking

Swarm installs as one native binary per OS/arch (Go + the Rust runtime). Because volunteer and edge machines sit behind NAT and firewalls, the agent dials out to its regional rendezvous rather than being dialed into — no inbound ports, works anywhere. On connect it advertises its capabilities over gossip, and those capabilities are exactly what the planner places jobs against.

15

Two execution tiers

Trust and capability are the same axis (section 05), so the runtime a task gets is decided by the tier of the machine it lands on. Untrusted open-tier machines run CPU workloads in a WASM sandbox — the one sandbox uniform across every OS — and because an untrusted machine can return wrong results, every open-tier result is confirmed by quorum across redundant runs. Trusted core-tier machines run native with GPUs, once — the only place barrier training and simulation can go, since WASM has no GPU path.

  • V1Open-tier results are quorum-verified before acceptance. An untrusted machine can lie or fault; run each task redundantly and accept only the agreed answer.
  • V2GPU and tight jobs are core-tier only. WASM has no production GPU path, so training and simulation run native on trusted machines.
  • V3Sensitive data never leaves the core tier. Anything confidential stays on trusted infrastructure; the open tier only ever sees non-sensitive, verifiable work.
Part III · Operate & Ship
16

Failure modes and operations

The whole architecture is a set of containment boundaries: a failure is absorbed at the level it happens and never escalates outward. A worker dying is a cell's problem; a cell dying is a region's problem; a region dying is the fleet's problem — and each outer level barely notices the inner one.

Self-registration and the reconnect loop

Machines join themselves — the swarm never reaches in. An agent dials out to its regional rendezvous and retries with backoff and jitter, forever, failing over to another region if its own is unreachable (the jitter prevents a thundering herd when a region recovers). It enrolls once for a SPIFFE identity, joins a cell, and stays a member via gossip heartbeats. If the connection drops it simply resumes the dial loop — keeping its identity — and the agent is crash-only, so a restart just re-runs the same loop.

Operating at fleet scale

Two things change when the fleet is a million machines. Observability can't scrape per node — metrics are pre-aggregated at the cell and rolled up per region, so cardinality stays bounded. Upgrades roll by draining: the agent is a single binary, so a cell is cordoned, its jobs checkpoint-migrate or drain, and the new version rolls in — gRPC/protobuf keeps mixed versions compatible during the roll. Machine maintenance is the same primitive: cordon, drain, done.

  • O1Observe at the cell, not the node. Pre-aggregate per cell and per region; per-node scraping does not survive a million machines.
  • O2Upgrade and maintain by draining. Cordon a cell, let its jobs checkpoint-migrate, roll the binary; protocol compatibility covers the version skew mid-roll.
  • O3Agents self-register and never give up. Dial-out with backoff and jitter, retry forever, fail over across regions; identity is kept across reconnects.
  • O4Detection is adaptive by tier; recovery is automatic. Fast eviction for tight core-tier jobs, patient timeouts for the flaky open tier; the planner reschedules failed jobs itself, no human in the loop.
17

Security model

Every machine that joins gets a cryptographic identity from the control-plane CA, built on SPIFFE/SPIRE. It presents an enrollment token — operator-issued for core-tier machines, anonymous self-serve for the open tier — gets an mTLS certificate, and from then on every connection (rendezvous, planner, cell gossip) is mutually authenticated and encrypted. The trust tier is baked into the identity, so the planner gates placement on it.

What a malicious participant can actually do

A participant is only a worker: it pulls a task, runs it, returns a result, and gossips its own health. So a hostile machine's whole repertoire is three moves — lie about its result, read what it's handed, and fake many identities (a Sybil attack, below). The architecture shrinks even that: because open-tier jobs are independent, workers never talk to each other, and untrusted machines are never placed in tight cells alongside others — so a bad machine has no peer to attack and its blast radius is its own single task. Sensitive data never reaches it (V3), and gossip is authenticated, so it can only ever speak for itself. That is why the threat model is pragmatic: the real risk is a machine lying about its own work, possibly at scale.

Sybil resistance without accounts

A Sybil attack is one attacker spinning up many fake identities to overpower a system that assumes one identity is one participant — here, to win a quorum vote and force a wrong result. You cannot stop it with self-reported hardware IDs: a MAC address is trivially spoofed, invisible across the internet, and fresh per VM or container — anything an untrusted machine says about itself can be forged. Real hardware binding needs TPM / secure-enclave attestation (via SPIRE node attestation), which we use on the core tier but can't rely on for random open-tier machines. So the open tier doesn't try to enforce one-identity-per-machine — it makes extra identities useless:

  • QQuorum + random assignment. Each task runs on K machines chosen randomly from the whole pool; forcing a wrong answer needs a large fraction of total compute, not a few fake nodes.
  • RReputation gating. A new anonymous identity starts at zero trust and is always cross-checked; trust is earned only by agreeing with quorum over time — so every Sybil must independently do honest work.
  • HHoneypot spot-checks. Occasionally hand out a task whose answer is known; a lie gets the identity blacklisted. No account required.
  • POptional proof-of-work to enroll. A small puzzle puts a cost floor under mass identity creation.

18

Deliberately not building this

The core is four moving parts. Everything below is a tempting complication that the threshold-and-declaration model makes unnecessary — each one buys cost and fragility without buying the requirements.

Continuous rebalancing

A control loop chasing "optimum" cell sizes pays the tear-down cost constantly. Split/merge at thresholds gets stability for free.

Cross-cell consensus

Any global agreement reintroduces the O(N²) / 5–7-voter wall. Cells coordinate only through async messages, never a shared vote.

Placement optimization

Elaborate bin-packing across a million nodes. The coupling field plus "fill until 2T" is enough; measure before adding a solver.

19

Phased roadmap

The sequence proves a working, geo-distributed swarm on easy workloads first, then adds capability, openness, scale, and finally self-tuning — each phase gated by a concrete exit criterion. It stays inside the non-goals of section 18: the later phases refine what we designed, never a continuous-rebalancing optimizer or a heavy placement solver.

P0

Core loop + easy mitosis single region · trusted · native

Agent self-registration (dial-out, backoff/jitter) · gossip membership · split/merge in the independent case · planner → queue → pull · CLI. Templates: keyspace-search, monte-carlo.

Exit — an independent job runs on a self-scaling single-region fleet and returns correct results; cells split and merge under load.

P1

Multi-region reach still independent

Regional control planes · per-region rendezvous · retry-forever + cross-region failover · thin global routing · cross-region job spread. Plus the throwaway barrier spike.

Exit — the fleet spans two or more regions, a job spreads across them, agents fail over between regions, and barrier mechanics are proven in the spike.

P2

Tight coupling + full driver set core tier

Per-cell Raft leader · barrier, leader, message-passing drivers · checkpointing · mitosis-at-checkpoint (B3) · gang scheduling · GPU · adaptive-by-tier detection. Templates: dist-training, sci-sim, graph-compute, agent-sim.

Exit — all nine templates run on the core tier; a GPU training job completes with checkpoint recovery after an induced failure; a barrier cell splits cleanly at a checkpoint boundary.

P3

Open / untrusted tier

WASM (Wasmtime) sandbox · result verification (quorum + random assignment) · reputation gating · honeypot spot-checks · anonymous SPIFFE/SPIRE enrollment · workload signing.

Exit — untrusted machines run independent jobs; quorum rejects an injected wrong result; a honeypot-caught liar is de-weighted; anonymous enrollment issues a valid identity.

P4

Scale infrastructure

FoundationDB registry · observability aggregation (cell/region rollup) · rolling upgrades by drain · backpressure / rate-limiting.

Exit — control plane and registry sustain target load with no single bottleneck; a rolling upgrade completes with zero job loss; metric cardinality stays bounded at fleet scale.

P5

Production readiness / GA

Multi-tenancy (quotas, fairness, isolation) · disaster recovery + chaos testing · SLAs and alerting · optional TPM/attestation on the core tier · security audit.

Exit — tenant quotas and fairness are enforced; a DR drill recovers a lost region; the security review passes; SLA targets are met in staging.

P6

Self-tuning + full-scale GA full design realized

Signal-based adaptive mitosis (split on measured p99, the §03 upgrade) · capability/locality-aware placement refinement (short of a solver, per §18) · reputation maturity · sustained 1M-node validation.

Exit — mitosis splits on a measured signal, placement honors capability and locality, and a sustained run at ~1M nodes is stable. The design is realized.
